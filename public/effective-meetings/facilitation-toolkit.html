Facilitating Meetings

The Facilitation Toolkit

Simple facilitation tools for everyday meetings, grouped by the zones of the Diamond Model: business as usual, divergent zone, groan zone, convergent zone and closure.

9 min read Facilitation Meetings

What this resource is for

A great deal of everyday leadership work happens inside meetings, and the quality of those meetings depends on a small number of learnable choices. This resource describes simple facilitation tools and when to use them. Their value comes from consistent use, not from complexity.

The tools are grouped by the zones of the Diamond Model of participatory decision-making: business as usual, the divergent zone, the groan zone, the convergent zone and closure. Each zone has a guiding question that you can ask the group as it is. Many tools work in more than one zone, so treat the grouping as a starting point, not a rule.

Business as usual

In business as usual, the group works on familiar topics and the facilitator sets the frame for the discussion. The guiding question is: “What exactly do we need to decide, and by when?”

Setting expectations means being explicit, at the start of a meeting, about what kind of meeting this is and what is expected of the people in the room. This includes the meeting's goal, but also practical things: whether this is a working session or a briefing, whether disagreement is welcome or the group is finalising something already decided, how long it will run, and what a good outcome looks like. Most wasted meeting time comes from participants working with different, unstated assumptions about what kind of conversation they are in. A short framing at the start, often no more than two sentences, prevents a lot of friction later.

An agenda lists the topics of a meeting in order, with the goal and the time available for each topic. A useful agenda item names the outcome, for example “Agree which features move to Q1”, not only the subject, for example “Roadmap”. Sending the agenda in advance lets people prepare, and it lets people who are not needed for a topic decide not to attend.

Roles make clear who does what in the meeting. Common roles are the facilitator, who guides the process, the content owner, who is responsible for the topic or the decision, the timekeeper and the person who takes notes. When the facilitator is also the content owner, it is hard to stay neutral, so separating the two roles helps in meetings where a decision is needed.

Timeboxing means assigning a fixed, visible time limit to an agenda item or a discussion, and holding to it even if the topic does not feel finished. A timebox is not a way to rush people. It makes the group prioritise early, since a group that knows it has fifteen minutes for a topic behaves differently from a group that believes it has as long as it needs. When a timebox runs out and the topic is not resolved, the honest options are to extend it deliberately and visibly, to park the remainder for a follow-up conversation, or to close it with whatever has been decided so far. A timebox should never quietly disappear, with the meeting running long without anyone naming it.

A check-in is a short round at the start of a meeting in which each person says one or two sentences, for example how they arrive, what they need from the meeting or what is on their mind. It helps people to focus on the meeting, it gives everyone a voice early, and it shows the facilitator what the group needs before the work starts.

Agreeing the decision rule means deciding how the group will decide before the discussion about the content begins. Options include a decision by one person after consultation, a majority vote and consent. When the rule is clear in advance, nobody can question the process after the result is known, and people can see how much influence they have.

Divergent zone

In the divergent zone, the group collects as many ideas and perspectives as possible, without judging them yet. The guiding question is: “What else?”

Brainstorming is the open, spoken generation of ideas on a question, with criticism postponed until the collection is complete. It is fast and energising, but the first ideas and the most confident voices tend to shape everything that follows, so it works best in groups that already trust each other.

Brainwriting asks everyone to write their ideas down silently before anyone speaks. It removes the anchoring effect of whoever speaks first, and it gives people who think better in writing than out loud a fair chance to contribute.

1-2-4-all structures a discussion in stages. Participants think alone for a minute, then pair up to compare notes, then join another pair to form a group of four, before the whole room hears a short summary from each group of four. It surfaces more ideas than moving straight to open discussion, and nobody can stay silent for the whole exercise.

Round robin means giving each person a turn to speak once, in a fixed order, before opening the floor to general discussion. It stops one or two voices from dominating a conversation and gives quieter participants time to speak without having to compete for it.

Small groups of three to five people work on a question in parallel and then report back to the whole group. Everyone gets more time to speak, and people often say things in a small group that they would not say in front of everyone. Groups can work on the same question to compare results, or on different parts of a problem to cover more ground.

Mind mapping places the central question in the middle of a page or board and adds ideas as branches around it, with related ideas on the same branch. It shows how ideas connect, and the visible gaps between branches invite the question “What else?”.

Groan zone

In the groan zone, perspectives collide and the discussion becomes uncomfortable. The facilitator helps people understand why their views differ before the group tries to agree. The guiding question is: “Where exactly do we disagree?”

Paraphrasing means repeating in your own words what someone has said, then checking whether you understood it correctly. It shows the speaker that they have been heard, it slows the discussion down when it gets heated, and it often reveals that two people who seemed to disagree mean the same thing.

Drawing people out means inviting someone to say more about their point with open questions such as “Can you say more about that?” or “What makes this important to you?”. It helps people who are struggling to explain a thought, and it shows the group that every contribution deserves attention.

Legitimising differences means stating openly that different perspectives are valid and expected, for example “It makes sense that Sales and Engineering see this differently, because they are measured on different things.” It reduces the pressure to win the argument and turns the disagreement into information the group can use.

Circular questions ask a person about the perspective of someone else, for example “How do you think Product sees this?” or “What would Operations say if they were in the room?”. They help people to step out of their own position and make the relationships between perspectives visible.

The perfection game is a structured way to give feedback on an idea or proposal. Each person rates it on a scale from 1 to 10, says what they like about it, and then says what it would take to make it a 10. Because the feedback always comes with a concrete improvement, criticism becomes a contribution instead of an attack. The technique comes from the Core Protocols by Jim and Michele McCarthy.

Ritual dissent tests an idea by exposing it to strong criticism in a safe, ritual form. A spokesperson presents the idea to another group and then turns their back, while the group gives only critical feedback and the spokesperson listens without replying. Because the criticism is part of a ritual and not personal, people can be more direct than usual, and the idea becomes stronger. The method was developed by Dave Snowden.

Convergent zone

In the convergent zone, the group combines, evaluates and narrows down its options. The guiding question is: “Which criteria matter most?”

Clustering means grouping similar ideas or sticky notes together and giving each group a short name. It turns a long, unordered list into a few themes that the group can discuss and compare.

Dot voting gives each participant a fixed number of marks, physical dots or digital votes, to place against a list of options. It quickly turns a long list into a visible order of priority, without debating every item in turn. Dot voting shows preferences; it does not replace a decision.

A criteria matrix lists the options in rows and the criteria in columns, and the group rates each option against each criterion. Agreeing the criteria, and how much each one counts, before rating the options keeps the discussion focused on what matters and makes the reasoning behind the result visible.

Gradients of agreement replace a yes or no with a scale that shows the level of support, from full endorsement through “I can live with it” and “I have reservations” to a block. The scale, described by Sam Kaner, shows whether a proposal has strong support or only weak acceptance before the group commits to it.

Bridging questions draw together what has been said and help the group move from separate positions towards something it can share, for example “What themes do we keep coming back to?” or “Where do we already agree, even if we have not said it?”.

The parking lot is a visible place, a flip chart or a shared document, for questions and topics that come up but do not belong in the current conversation. Writing them down, instead of ignoring them or following them immediately, protects the meeting's focus and still shows that the point mattered. Agree before the end of the meeting what happens to each item.

Closure

At closure, the group makes the decision explicit and agrees what happens next. The guiding question is: “Who does what, by when?”

Fist to five is a fast way to check the level of support for a decision before finalising it. Each person shows zero to five fingers, from no support at all to full support, and the facilitator can see at a glance whether the group agrees or whether a few low scores need to be discussed before moving on. It works well as the closing gesture for consent-based decisions.

Consent means that a proposal is accepted when nobody has a reasoned objection to it. The question is not “Is this the best option?” but “Can you live with this and support it, even if it is not your first choice?”. Consent lets a group move forward without requiring everyone to agree completely, and objections are treated as information to improve the proposal.

The decider protocol is a fast way to decide on a proposal. The proposer states the proposal, and everyone votes at the same time with yes, no or support. If only a few people vote no, the proposer asks each of them what it would take for them to vote yes, and can change the proposal. The protocol also comes from the Core Protocols by Jim and Michele McCarthy.

ROTI (Return on Time Invested) is a one-minute closing practice for any meeting. Each participant rates the meeting on a scale from one to five: 1 is a waste of time, 2 is not worth it, 3 is okay but could have been better, 4 is a good use of time, and 5 is excellent. Participants can hold up fingers, write a number on a sticky note or respond in the chat. The facilitator then asks briefly what would have made a 4 a 5, or what made a low score low. ROTI is fast, it shows patterns over time, and it gives quieter participants a low-pressure way to signal a problem with the meeting itself.

SMART actions are agreed actions that are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound, each with one named owner. Writing actions in this form at the end of a meeting makes it clear who does what by when, and it makes follow-up in the next meeting easy.

Further reading

  • Lipmanowicz, H. and McCandless, K.: The Surprising Power of Liberating Structures. The source of 1-2-4-all and many other simple formats for structuring participation.
  • Kaner, S., Lind, L., Toldi, C., Fisk, S., and Berger, D.: Facilitator's Guide to Participatory Decision-Making. The source of the Diamond Model, gradients of agreement and many of the groan zone techniques.
  • McCarthy, J. and McCarthy, M.: Software for Your Head. Describes the Core Protocols, including the perfection game and the decider protocol.
