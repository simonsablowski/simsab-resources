Resource 02 · Conflict Navigation

Constructive Disagreement and Feedback

Why feedback conversations fail and how to avoid the common failure modes. The SARA model: understanding the emotional arc of receiving difficult feedback. Non-violent communication: observations, feelings, needs, and requests. A practical structure for delivering and receiving feedback.

12 min read Feedback Conflict Communication

What this resource is for

As AI accelerates delivery, the conversations that were already uncomfortable become more frequent and more consequential. Misalignment that previously had weeks to surface now needs to be caught in days. Feedback that was deferred indefinitely now has a real cost. The skills covered in this resource — giving and receiving feedback, having difficult conversations constructively — are not soft skills in an AI-accelerated environment. They are coordination infrastructure.

Knowing that a conflict exists and understanding its structure is necessary but not sufficient. At some point, someone has to have the conversation. This resource covers the skills and models that make difficult feedback conversations productive: understanding the emotional arc of receiving hard news, structuring feedback so it is heard rather than defended against, and communicating in a way that preserves the relationship while still addressing the substance.

Why feedback matters and why it is difficult

Feedback is essential for growth as a person, a team, a product, or an organisation. It is not always easy to accept, however. When you are not used to receiving feedback, it can make you feel uncomfortable. Feedback can hurt, especially when it is interpreted as a critique of your person rather than a suggestion for improvement. This can lead people to avoid feedback or reject it even when it would genuinely help them.

Feedback comes in many forms: performance conversations, peer observations, mentoring, retrospectives, or even a failed project that signals something about how the work is being done. What all useful feedback has in common is that it is actionable, specific, and aimed at behaviour rather than character. Most people give feedback with positive intent — they want the other person to succeed. That intent does not automatically make the feedback well-delivered, well-timed, or well-received. Both giving and receiving feedback require skill, and skill requires practice.

Why feedback conversations fail

Difficult conversations fail for a small number of predictable reasons. The most common is that the person giving feedback conflates the observable behaviour with a judgment about the other person's character or intent. "You were late to three meetings" is an observation; "you don't respect other people's time" is an attribution of intent. Attributions trigger defensiveness immediately, because the other person experiences them — often correctly — as an unfair characterisation of who they are.

A second common failure is timing. Feedback delivered in the heat of a moment, or in front of others, is almost never received well. When emotions are high, the receiver has tunnel vision rather than being able to calmly process what is being said. Equally, waiting too long is a problem — feedback loses specificity and usefulness as memory fades. The general principle is to wait until both parties are in a position to listen, be present, and discuss calmly, without waiting so long that the moment has passed.

A third failure is the absence of an established relationship. When the person giving feedback has not built sufficient trust or credibility with the receiver, the feedback is likely to feel inappropriate regardless of its content. As a rule of thumb, feedback should not be given when it has not been asked for and the relationship does not yet support it. When in doubt, ask the other person whether they are open to hearing your perspective before offering it.

A fourth failure is the absence of specificity. Vague feedback ("you need to communicate better") gives the receiver nothing to act on, and often reads as a verdict rather than a direction. Specific, behavioural feedback gives someone something concrete to engage with.

The SARA model: understanding the emotional arc

When someone receives feedback that is critical, challenging, or unexpected, they typically move through a predictable emotional sequence before they are able to genuinely engage with the content. This sequence is described by the SARA model. Knowing the model is useful in both directions — as a receiver it helps you name what you are experiencing; as a giver it helps you not mistake early-stage reactions for final responses.

Surprise is the first response to unexpected feedback. The person is caught off guard. Even if the feedback is accurate and well-delivered, the initial response is often a kind of defensiveness or disconnection. Nothing much can be processed at this stage.

Anger follows when the surprise resolves into a reactive emotion. The person may feel attacked, misunderstood, or treated unfairly. They are likely to push back, deflect, or look for flaws in the feedback. This is not bad faith — it is a normal defensive response to perceived threat.

Resistance is the stage where the person actively pushes back against the feedback to protect their self-image. They may make excuses, question the validity of the data or the person giving it, or agree outwardly without any genuine intention to change. This is the hardest stage to move past, because it requires accepting a truth that is uncomfortable. It often happens away from the original conversation — after sleeping on it — and may not be visible to the feedback giver at all.

Acceptance is the stage where the person is able to see the feedback with some objectivity and make a choice: to act on it, or to reject it. This is the only stage at which genuine learning and change become possible. Importantly, acceptance is a choice — not all feedback deserves to be acted on, and the receiver should carefully consider what they take on board and what they set aside.

The practical implication of the SARA model is that feedback conversations are almost never resolved in a single exchange. A person who leaves a difficult conversation in the Anger or Resistance stage has not permanently rejected the feedback — they may simply need time and space to move through the arc. Following up a day or two later often produces a very different conversation.

How to use SARA when receiving feedback. The most powerful aspect of the model is recognising that moving through these stages is a normal human reaction, not a personal failing. When you receive tough feedback and notice shock, anger, or the urge to resist, name the stage to yourself: "I'm in the Anger stage — I don't need to respond right now." Avoid reacting immediately while in the Surprise, Anger, or Resistance stages — your judgment will be clouded and a defensive response rarely serves you. A simple "Thank you for the feedback, I need some time to think about it" is a legitimate and often ideal response. When you have moved past the strongest emotions, return to the feedback and listen with the goal of understanding rather than defending. And allow yourself time — do not try to force yourself to Acceptance immediately.

Non-violent communication

Non-violent communication (NVC) is a structured approach to interpersonal communication developed by Marshall Rosenberg. It is designed to keep conversations focused on concrete reality and genuine needs, rather than on evaluations, judgments, and demands. In organisational settings, it is particularly useful in feedback and conflict conversations where there is already some emotional charge.

NVC organises communication around four elements, always in this sequence.

Observations are specific, factual descriptions of what happened, stripped of evaluation. The test is whether a camera could have captured it. "In the last three planning sessions, you arrived more than ten minutes after the start time" is an observation. "You're always late" is a judgment dressed as an observation. The distinction matters enormously, because evaluations invite argument while observations invite engagement. In escalated conflicts in particular, people tend to confuse facts, opinions, prejudices, and feelings. Separating these out is difficult but vital.

Feelings are the emotional response that the observation triggered in you. NVC distinguishes carefully between feelings and interpretations. "I feel ignored" is actually an interpretation — it contains a theory about what the other person intended. "I feel frustrated" or "I feel anxious about our shared deadlines" is a genuine feeling statement. Feelings are not signs that something is right or wrong — they are neutral data that point toward unmet needs. Being transparent about feelings and demonstrating some vulnerability, though uncomfortable, is key to building the kind of understanding that makes resolution possible.

Needs are the underlying values or requirements that your feelings are pointing to. The cause of our feelings can generally be tied to needs that have not been met or have been violated — the need to be accepted, trusted, respected, or recognised, among many others. When it is clear which needs have been neglected, it becomes possible to examine how to fulfil those needs and, thus, move toward resolution. Naming the need rather than continuing to focus on the behaviour shifts the conversation from blame to understanding.

Requests are specific, concrete, and positive — they describe an action you are asking for, not a behaviour you want to stop. "Would you be willing to message the group by 8:45 if you know you'll be delayed?" is a request. "Stop being late" is a demand. While demands create a power struggle with only submission or rebellion as options, requests are concrete suggestions for future interaction. They give the other party something they can do to help improve the situation and build trust. Requests invite agency; demands generate compliance or resistance.

In practice, you do not need to use NVC as a rigid script. The four-element structure is most useful as a preparation tool: before a difficult conversation, work through what the observations actually are, what you genuinely feel, what need is underneath that feeling, and what specific change you are asking for. That preparation alone will significantly improve the quality of the conversation you actually have.

Structuring a feedback conversation

A practical structure for a difficult feedback conversation in a professional context has five phases.

Opening sets the intention. Be direct about the purpose of the conversation without being aggressive. "I want to talk about something that's been creating friction for me, and I'd like us to find a way through it together" is an honest opening that signals partnership rather than accusation.

Observation shares the specific behaviour you observed, using the NVC distinction between fact and evaluation. Stay with what you saw or experienced — do not venture into what you think it means about the person.

Impact shares the effect the behaviour had — on you, on the team, on the work. This is not blame; it is information. Many people are genuinely unaware of the downstream effects of their behaviour. Sharing impact without attributing intent gives the other person something to engage with rather than defend against.

Inquiry asks a genuine question before moving to requests or solutions. "What was going on for you in those meetings?" or "Is there something I'm not seeing from your side?" This is not a rhetorical move — it is an acknowledgment that you may have an incomplete picture, and that the other person's perspective is relevant to finding a workable solution.

Forward movement — only once the other person has had space to respond — is where you make a specific, actionable request. If the conversation has gone well, this often becomes a collaborative problem-solving moment rather than a unilateral demand.

The VIBE framework: observing what is really being communicated

One of the most common failure modes in feedback and difficult conversations is taking what someone says at face value while missing what they are actually communicating. People do not always express their real concerns directly — sometimes because they are not fully aware of them, and sometimes because the social context makes it difficult. The VIBE framework is a practical tool for observing the consistency between what someone says and how they say it, across four simultaneous channels.

Voice covers paraverbal communication — not the words themselves, but the tone, volume, speaking speed, and pauses. A voice that trembles, accelerates, or goes flat can signal tension, uncertainty, or disengagement even when the words express the opposite.

Information is the verbal content: the facts, data, and logic of what is being said. This is the "what." The observer notes whether the information is clear, vague, or internally contradictory — and whether it is consistent with what was said previously.

Body language is the non-verbal channel: posture, gestures, facial expressions, and eye contact. The body often communicates what the mind is trying to conceal. Someone claiming openness while sitting with crossed arms and avoiding eye contact is sending two different signals simultaneously.

Emotions is the overall energy or climate the person is emanating — the underlying emotional tone that colours the entire interaction. It is the synthesis of how the other three channels combine. A person might describe a situation using neutral words, but the emotional atmosphere they create might be one of anxiety, frustration, or resignation.

The main practical value of VIBE is identifying inconsistencies. If the information is positive ("I'm fine with this decision") but the voice is flat and the body language is withdrawn, the perceived emotion tells a different story. Noticing these discrepancies gives you the opportunity to ask a more targeted question rather than accepting a surface-level answer and moving on — which often means the real issue stays unaddressed.

Receiving feedback

Constructive disagreement is not only a skill for the person giving feedback. Receiving feedback well — especially unexpected or critical feedback — is equally important and equally learnable.

The most important practice is to delay evaluation. When you receive feedback that stings or surprises you, resist the immediate impulse to agree, disagree, or explain. Simply listen and ask clarifying questions. "Can you say more about what you observed?" or "When specifically did that happen?" are not defensive moves — they are ways of gathering information before you respond.

It also helps to proactively seek feedback from people whose opinion you respect, about specific behaviours or aspects of your work that you want to improve. Being proactive reduces the sense of surprise, and choosing trusted people reduces the anger response. Requesting feedback in advance also allows the giver to formulate their observations carefully, rather than relying on an imprecise recollection.

Remember that not all feedback deserves to be accepted. Consider carefully what you take on board and what you set aside. And when someone takes the time and courage to give you feedback — thank them for it, regardless of whether you agree.
